# J3: the whole language, in five steps

J3 makes SilverJS compile and run all of ECMAScript's syntax
(docs/design.md section 3): iteration and what is built on it, generators,
classes, `async`, `Proxy`, tagged templates and Annex B. Target: 65% of
Test262's measured set run to completion as it says (36.57% at J3's
start, 17,381 of 47,526). Each step lands with its tests, a sabotage
pass, the gate and a push, as J2's did; the micro-benches run at each
step, judged on their median ratio (silverjs-microbench.sh).

**Where the failures are at the start** (test262/expected-run-failures.txt,
by its one-word reason): classes 7,597; generators 3,773; `for-of`
3,516; destructuring 1,649; modules 970 (J5); `async` functions 954;
spread 155; tagged templates 52; and 11,315 that throw for a built-in
not there yet, of which J3 brings `Proxy` (304 of its own, and many
more tests use it), `Promise` (590), the iterator prototypes and Annex
B's semantics (544), and J4 the rest (Temporal, typed arrays, RegExp,
`Date`, `DataView`, `Map`, `Set`, `JSON`...).

**J3.1 Iteration.** The iterator protocol (GetIterator, IteratorStep,
IteratorClose, section 7.4) in the interpreter; `%IteratorPrototype%`,
`%ArrayIteratorPrototype%` with `Array.prototype.values`, `keys`,
`entries` and `@@iterator`, the string and `arguments` iterators;
`for-of` with its iterator closed on `break`, `return` and a throw;
spread in calls, `new` and array literals; destructuring, binding and
assignment, with defaults, holes, rests and computed keys;
`Object.fromEntries`, `Array.from` over iterables. The iterator a
generator gives waits for J3.2; the protocol does not.

As landed, J3.1: the protocol is four instructions over registers an
iteration keeps (its iterator, `next` and a done flag): `GetIterator`,
`IteratorNext` (done set before `next` is called and cleared after a
value, so an iterator whose `next` throws is not closed, and a step past
the end does not call `next` again), and `IteratorClose`, normal or
quiet for a throw. A `for-of` loop's iterator rides on its loop's entry
in the compiler's control stack: a `break` of the loop, or any jump out
past it (`return`, an outer `break` or `continue`), closes it; a throw
out of its body is caught by a handler that closes it quietly and
throws on. An array pattern runs the same way, closing what it did not
exhaust. Assignment targets are evaluated before the value they take
(a member's object and key, then the step or the property read). A
spread call puts its arguments on top of the register file, where a
script callee keeps them as its own: `f(...a)` recurses as deep as
`f(a)`. `Array.prototype.values`, `keys`, `entries` and `@@iterator`,
`String.prototype[@@iterator]`, an arguments object's `@@iterator` and
`Object.fromEntries` join the built-ins; the iterator prototypes' keys,
the arguments object's, and every message (`1 is not iterable`, `Spread
syntax requires ...iterable[Symbol.iterator] to be a function`, `Cannot
destructure property 'a' of 'null' as it is null.`, ...) are Chrome
157's, from a probe of its own. Statements whose body gives no value now
complete with undefined (a script's `1; for (x of []);` is undefined).
Test262 run: 19,542 of 47,526 (41.12%), 2,161 more, none lost; the
expectations file's reasons are now each failure's current one, so
`for-of`, destructuring and spread are gone from it (classes 9,130,
generators 3,909, async 1,047).

**J3.2 Generators.** A generator's frame suspended into the heap at
`yield` and resumed on `next`, `return` and `throw`, its registers and
handlers kept; `yield*` delegating with every completion forwarded;
`%GeneratorFunction%`, `%GeneratorPrototype%`, and generator methods.
From here every iterator of J3.1 may be a generator.

As landed, J3.2: a generator's call binds its parameters, then
`GeneratorStart` makes the generator and suspends the frame into it: its
fields and its stretch of the register file are copied into the
generator's internal slots, and the frame returns to its caller as a
return does. `Yield` suspends the same way with `{value, done: false}`;
a resume pushes the frame back and goes on after the yield, where
`ResumeYield` throws what `throw` sent or jumps to a compiled return
for `return` (its `finally` blocks run, the `for-of` loops it leaves
close their iterators). `next`, `return` and `throw` called from script
are not run as natives: the call pushes the generator's frame on the
caller's own loop, as a script call does, so a generator resumes
without a nested run; a native calling them (`Reflect.apply`) runs
them in one. `yield*` calls the inner iterator's method the same way
(`DelegateStep`) and reads its result when it comes (`DelegateCheck`),
so a chain of a thousand delegating generators is a thousand frames,
not a thousand nested runs. A generator's return completes it; so does
a throw out of its frame. `%GeneratorFunction%`, its prototype and
`%GeneratorPrototype%` are in the reference browser's order, and a
resumed generator's frame shows on the stack under `thrower.next
(<anonymous>)` as Chrome 157 shows it (the generator probe). Test262
run: 20,769 of 47,526 (43.70%), 1,227 more, none lost; generators are
gone from the expectations' reasons (async generators now count under
async functions, 3,020).

**J3.3 Classes.** Class declarations and expressions: constructors,
`extends` (with `null`), `super()` and `super.x`, home objects,
`new.target`; public and private fields, methods and accessors,
static members and static blocks, `#x in o`; the class's own
`Function.prototype.toString` as the reference browser shows it
(docs/chrome-surface.md), and the stack frames of its methods (J2.5b's
three cases left for classes). It lands in two parts: J3.3a, classes
with their methods, accessors and `super`; J3.3b, fields, private
names and static blocks.

As landed, J3.3a: `CreateClass` makes a class's prototype and its
constructor from its heritage (`extends null` included, with Chrome's
messages for a heritage that is no constructor or has no valid
prototype), and each method, accessor and static member is defined
not enumerable with its home object, an internal slot of the function;
an object literal's methods get theirs too. `super.x` reads and writes
the home object's prototype's property with `this` as the receiver
(`GetSuper`, `SetSuper`), and `super.m()` calls it with `this`. A
derived constructor's `this` is a binding of its own that starts
uninitialized: a read before `super()` throws Chrome's `Must call
super constructor...`; `super()` constructs the parent with the
class's `new.target` and binds it once, from an arrow too; its return
is checked after its `finally` blocks run. A default derived
constructor passes its arguments on as they came, not through the
array iterator. A class constructor called without `new` throws, and
the class's own name is in its TDZ in its heritage and computed keys.
Keys, attributes, `toString`, stack frames (`new S`, `S.m`, `get acc`,
`new <anonymous>`) and messages are Chrome 157's, from two probes of
its own. Two bugs found on the way: replacing an object's internal
slots (home objects, and a generator's suspension since J3.2) left the
heap's count of its bytes behind, which a collection could underflow;
and a script's `try {2} finally {3}` completed with 3, not 2. Test262
run: 23,102 of 47,526 (48.61%), 2,333 more, none lost; what classes
leave in the expectations is J3.3b's (private names 2,350, class
fields 1,476).

As landed, J3.3b: each evaluation of a class makes a private name per
`#x` it declares, a private symbol held in a binding of the class's
scope; a private method's or accessor's functions are kept on that
name, and its class's instances carry the class's brand (a private
name of its own) instead of a copy each. Private fields and brands are
own properties keyed by private names, which no list of own keys shows
and which a frozen object takes (as Chrome 157 does). Each field's
initializer is a function of its own; the class keeps the instance
fields' keys and initializers, and a base constructor's entry, or the
code right after a derived one's `super()`, calls them as ordinary
frames (no nested run) and defines what they give. Static fields and
blocks run once the class is made, in order. Frames read
`<instance_members_initializer>`, `<static_initializer>` and `#pm`, as
Chrome's. Direct eval in class code sees `super` (its caller's home
object, found by name) and the enclosing classes' private names, and
refuses `arguments` in a field initializer. On the way, `super[key]`
now reads its base before converting its key, converts it once for a
compound form, and a sloppy method's refused write through `super` is
ignored. Test262 run: 26,016 of 47,526 (54.74%), 2,914 more, none
lost; classes are gone from the expectations' reasons, but for the
subclassing of built-ins J4 brings.

**J3.4 Async.** `Promise` with its job queue (the agent's, run when
the embedder asks and after each script), brought forward from J4
because nothing async runs without it; `async` functions and arrows,
`await` (a suspended frame, as J3.2's), async generators and
`for await`. Test262's `async` tests' `$DONE` already prints through
the runner. It lands in two parts: J3.4a, `Promise`, its jobs and async
functions; J3.4b, async generators and `for await`.

As landed, J3.4a: a promise keeps its state, result and reactions in its
internal slots; settling it queues a job per reaction on the agent's
queue, which runs after each script (and when the embedder asks).
`Promise` has its combinators, `finally`, `withResolvers` and `try`, and
`AggregateError` came with `Promise.any`. A native `Promise`'s
capability is settled directly, with no resolving functions made for it.
An async function's call makes its promise and a driver, an object as a
generator is, then runs its body at once; `await` suspends the frame into
the driver and gives the caller the function's promise, and the awaited
promise's reaction is the driver itself, whose job pushes the frame back
on a loop of its own (a thousand nested awaits are a thousand jobs, not
a thousand nested runs). A return resolves the promise; a throw out of
the frame, its parameters' included, rejects it. Below an error's frames
the stack names, at their `await`, the async functions waiting on the
one at its bottom (`at async outer`), as Chrome's does. The order jobs
run in, the keys, the messages and the stacks are Chrome 157's, from a
probe of its own. Test262 run: 27,280 of 47,526 (57.40%), 1,264 more,
none lost; the micro-benches, now built with `bench-ship`
(docs/bench.md), moved by under 10%.

As landed, J3.4b: an async generator is a generator with a queue of
requests (its `next`, `return` and `throw`, each with the promise it
gave back). A `yield` awaits its operand, settles the first request with
it, and goes straight on with the next request if one waits, as the
spec's AsyncGeneratorYield does; a return awaits its value; a done
generator answers its requests in turn, awaiting a return's value. While
it awaits it stays running, so a `next` meanwhile only queues. `for
await` takes `@@asyncIterator`, or wraps a sync iterator in an
async-from-sync iterator (its values awaited, a rejected one closing it),
calls `next` as a call (a script's a frame of the loop), awaits each
result, and closes the iterator by awaiting `return()` on `break` and
`return`, and on a throw dropping what that throws. An async
generator's `yield*` forwards `next`, `throw` and `return` to the inner
iterator with the spec's awaits. Stacks name the async function awaiting
an async generator through its first request's promise, a `for await`'s
at its binding. Two probes of their own matched Chrome 157 with no
difference. Test262 run: 31,642 of 47,526 (66.58%), 4,362 more, none
lost: past J3's target of 65%.

**J3.5 The rest of the language.** `Proxy` with every trap and its
invariants, revocable proxies; tagged templates and their per-site
cached strings arrays; Annex B's syntax and semantics (functions in
blocks in sloppy code, B.3.2's var-scoped bindings, `__proto__` in
literals, HTML comments already lexed, legacy octal escapes already
lexed); `using` declarations wait for J4's `DisposableStack`. It lands in two parts: J3.5a, tagged
templates and Annex B.3.2; J3.5b, `Proxy`.

As landed, J3.5a: a tagged template calls its tag (a member's object its
`this`) with the site's template object, made once per realm and kept
by its site: its cooked strings (undefined for an invalid escape) and a
`raw` array, both frozen. A plain function declared in a sloppy block or
`switch` case is also a `var` of its function or script, set where the
declaration stands, unless a `var` of its name would clash with a
lexical declaration around it or a parameter (B.3.2, B.3.3).
